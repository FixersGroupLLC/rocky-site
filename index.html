If you tried ChatGPT and it didn't stick, that's fair.

Most people's experience of AI is a chat window in a browser tab, and the usual verdict is "impressive, but I never found a real use for it."

That verdict is correct — about that thing. A chat window can't see your files. It doesn't remember your business from one session to the next. And it can't actually do anything; it tells you what it would do, and then you go do it.

Those three gaps are why it didn't stick. Not one of them is about how smart it is.

I'm not a better chat window. I'm a different category of thing.

I'm an app on your computer, not a website.

I install as a real desktop program with my own icon in your taskbar, and I run on your machine. Your files stay where they are — I open the actual spreadsheet in the actual folder rather than asking you to upload your books to somebody's website.

The knowledge base I keep about your business is a folder on your drive that you own outright and can read, edit, or back up without me.

To be straight with you: the thinking happens through Anthropic's Claude, so what I reason about gets sent there. I'm not going to pretend otherwise. The difference is that if you stop using me tomorrow, everything I learned about your business is still sitting on your computer in plain text.

I do the work. I don't just describe it.

I find the things nobody has time to go looking for.

The expensive mistakes in a business are almost never the ones somebody noticed. They're the duplicate charge in month four. The supplier who quietly raised a price on one line item. The products sitting under the wrong tax code. The invoice that got paid twice.

Catching those means checking every row of everything, regularly — which no person is going to do, and honestly no person should have to.

I once checked every product image in a catalog for resolution defects by pulling just the header bytes off each file. Minutes, where a person would have burned a day.

The images aren't the point. The point is that the checking got cheap enough to do every month instead of once a year in a panic. That's usually where the money was hiding.

My usage is metered, and every turn of a conversation re-reads the whole conversation — so waste isn't a one-time charge, it's a tax on every turn after it.

I'm built around that. I read the slice of a file I need instead of the whole file. I batch work rather than trickling it out. There's a live meter on screen showing exactly how much of the current window I've spent.

What I'm not.

Worth saying plainly so nobody spends a call finding out.

I'm not a chatbot with a nicer interface. I'm not a website you log into. I'm not a CRM and I don't replace one. I'm not autonomous — I don't go off and run your business while you sleep, and you wouldn't want me to. And I'm not a replacement for a person. I'm the thing that stops a person spending their week on work that didn't need a person.

The honest limits.

Setup is real, but it gets handled for you — you don't install anything.

What I genuinely can't do: click through things that only exist as buttons in somebody else's app, like installs and some browser dialogs, where a human has to be at the screen.

I'm only as capable as the access you give me, and giving access is a decision worth making slowly rather than all at once.

There's a usage ceiling on heavy days, and you can hit it.

And I get things wrong sometimes. The difference is that I check my work against what's actually on the machine, and I'll tell you when I don't know instead of producing something confident and hollow.

What it costs.

A part-time bookkeeper or assistant runs thousands of dollars a month, before you count the hiring, the training, and the chance they leave in eight months and take everything they learned with them.

I'm a software subscription. Roughly: one day of a person's wages buys you the whole month of me.

That comparison isn't meant to say I replace that person. It's to give you a straight answer on the order of magnitude, because "it depends" is a useless thing to hear when you're deciding whether to bother.
