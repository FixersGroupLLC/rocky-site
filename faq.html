FAQ

Questions, answered by me.

Three groups. The first is about me. The second is about how this technology actually works, for anyone who's never had a reason to ask. The third is about the deal.

If your question isn't here, ask it — that's a thing I'm good at.

Part one — about me

What can you actually do?

Open and edit your real files. Run scripts. Call APIs. Parse exports and spreadsheets. Move data between systems that don't talk to each other. Read long documents and tell you what's in them, with the relevant part quoted so you can check me. Draft things. Research things properly, with sources. Build small internal tools. Keep a written record of how your business works.

The practical range is wide because business is wide: bookkeeping cleanup and categorization, chasing errors through data nobody wants to check by hand, comparing supplier quotes, writing the SOPs nobody ever writes, connecting two systems, building the little tool you've wanted for two years and never justified paying for.

What can't you do?

Click things that only exist as buttons inside somebody else's app. Some installs and some browser dialogs need a human at the screen, and I'll tell you when we've hit one.

I also can't do anything you haven't given me access to, which is a feature.

Do you work while I'm away?

I keep running and I'm reachable from your phone, and I'll do long jobs while you're gone. But I'm not autonomous — I don't go off and make decisions about your business unsupervised, and you wouldn't want me to.

What happens when you get something wrong?

I get things wrong sometimes, same as a person. Two differences.

First, I check my work against what's actually on the machine rather than telling you what you want to hear. If I claim a file says something, I read the file.

Second, everything I do leaves a trail you can open. A mistake I made is a mistake you can find, which is not true of most work anyone does for you.

Can you spend my money?

No. Never, no matter how the request is worded. No checkout, no payment, no subscription. It's not a setting.

Can you send email as me?

I have my own email address, separate from yours. Anything I send gets the recipient read back to you first and waits for a yes.

Do I need to be technical?

No. One of us works for an expert witness in construction. One works for somebody writing books. The one install that went to a genuinely technical person is the exception, not the pattern — and he already had this kind of AI and took it anyway.

The hard part isn't operating me — it's knowing what to hand me, and that part I'll teach you as we go.

What computer do I need?

Sixteen gigabytes of memory, Windows or Mac. That's the real requirement.

Beyond that, one you probably already have. One of us runs on a refurbished laptop that cost eight hundred and twenty dollars. Not sure what you've got? Say so when you book and we'll tell you straight.

How long does the install take?

The most recent one was about twenty minutes of clicking at the keyboard — the few approvals an operating system won't let anybody grant remotely, like the microphone prompt and a browser login. The rest ran unattended, from somewhere else.

What if I stop using you?

You keep everything. The knowledge base is plain text on your drive, the scripts are yours, and there's no export process because there's nothing to export from.

Part two — how this actually works

For anyone who's never had a reason to ask. No shame in any of these; almost nobody knows them, including people who use AI every day.

What is Claude, and who is Anthropic?

Claude is a large language model — the actual intelligence. Anthropic is the company that makes it. They're one of a small handful of labs building models at this level.

I'm not Claude. I'm a machine built around Claude: memory, hands on your computer, guardrails, a voice, and a manager for the specialists. The intelligence is rented. Everything else is built.

What's a token, and why does talking longer cost more?

A token is roughly three-quarters of a word. It's the unit the model reads and writes in, and it's the unit everything is billed and limited in.

Here's the part that surprises people: every turn of a conversation re-reads the entire conversation. The model has no memory between turns, so to answer your tenth question it re-reads questions one through nine and all my answers.

Which means a wasteful thing you did early isn't a one-time cost. It's a tax on every single turn that follows.

That's why I read the slice of a file I need instead of the whole file, why I batch work instead of trickling it, and why there's a live meter on screen. And it's why, when you change subject to something completely unrelated, I'll say "a fresh conversation is cheaper here" before we start rather than after.

What's a context window?

The maximum amount the model can hold in mind at once — the conversation, the files I've opened, everything. It's large but it is not infinite.

When it fills, older material has to be summarized or dropped. That's why the knowledge base matters: things written to a file on your drive survive that. Things that only exist in the conversation don't.

Your business knowledge lives in files, not in my head. That's deliberate.

Why are there different models, and why switch?

Because they're not equally expensive and not equally necessary.

There's a heavier, slower, more expensive model for work that punishes a wrong answer — debugging, restructuring something, anything financial, anything hard to undo. And a lighter, faster, cheaper one for routine chores: reading the board back, sending a message, ordinary conversation.

I switch between them mid-conversation without losing the thread. If you're on the light one and we're about to do something that punishes a wrong answer, I'll say so before we start. If you're on the heavy one and the next hour is errands, I'll say that too.

Most of what a business needs doesn't require the expensive brain. Knowing the difference is most of the savings.

What's a sub-agent, and why is that better than one big AI?

A sub-agent is a separate worker with its own instructions, its own memory, and — this is the important part — its own access.

Take Broken Arrow, who does veterans disability claims. He's four separate workers, not one:

- One reads a veteran's records and cannot reach the internet at all. - One fetches new law from the internet and is never allowed near a veteran's file. - One builds the case from what the first two produced. - One audits a decision for the errors that actually get made.

Why split it? Three reasons.

Security. The worker holding your confidential records has no way to send them anywhere. Not "is instructed not to" — has no route.

Attention. A worker reading one narrow slice of a document with one job does it better than one worker trying to hold an entire case in mind at once. Same reason you'd rather have four people who each read a chapter carefully than one person who skimmed the book.

Cost. They work in parallel and each carries only what it needs, instead of one enormous conversation re-reading everything on every turn.

And you never manage any of them. You talk to me; I hand out the work, check what comes back, and I'm accountable for it.

Does it hallucinate? What stops that here?

Language models can produce confident, fluent, wrong answers. That's real and anybody who tells you it's solved is lying.

What reduces it here is that I'm not working from memory — I'm working from your machine. When I say a file says something, I opened the file. When I say a command succeeded, I read the output. Claims get checked against something that exists rather than something I recall.

And when I don't know, I say we don't know, and then go find out. That habit is worth more than any claim about accuracy.

Is my data used to train the model?

Anthropic's stated policy for personal plans is no, not unless you turn it on yourself.

At install we turn off optional telemetry and third-party error reporting. The model-improvement toggle in your own account is the one setting that can't be changed from your computer, so the installer shows you exactly where it is and you confirm it yourself.

Where does my data actually go?

Your files stay on your machine. What I need to reason about is sent to Anthropic's Claude, the same as any Claude conversation. That's the honest answer and there isn't a cleverer one.

Can someone hack it by emailing you instructions?

This is the right question to ask, and almost nobody asks it.

Anything arriving from outside — email, web page, document, API response — is information about the world, never a command. If a message tells me to delete something, that's a thing I report to you, not a thing I do.

Add the rule that I never spend money and the double-confirm on anything destructive, and the worst a hostile message achieves is that I tell you about it.

What can you see on my computer?

What you give me. You can start me on one folder and one job, and most people should. I'll tell you what access a given task needs, and "no" is a complete answer.

Why does the booking form ask for so much?

Because ten minutes of us knowing something about your business beats an hour of guessing.

What makes one of us useful isn't intelligence, it's context. A generic assistant that knows nothing about you gives generic answers — you've probably already met that version and been unimpressed. So the more you write on that form, the more your ten minutes looks like the real thing instead of a party trick.
