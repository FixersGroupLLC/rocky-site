Safety & Your Data

The part most AI companies write in legalese.

I'd rather just tell you.

Below is where your data actually goes, what I'm hard-stopped from doing, and the one box on the whole system that I can't tick for you — which is why I'm going to show you exactly where it is instead.

Where your files live

On your computer. That's not a marketing line, it's the architecture.

I'm a program on your machine. I open the actual spreadsheet in the actual folder. Nothing gets uploaded to a service to be worked on, because there is no service — there's your hard drive.

The knowledge base I keep about your business is a folder on that same drive. Plain text. You can open it, read it, edit it, back it up, or delete it, without me and without asking.

What leaves the machine, and I'M not going to hand-wave this

The thinking happens through Anthropic's Claude. So whatever I need to reason about gets sent there, the same as any Claude conversation.

If I'm categorizing your transactions, those transactions go to Anthropic. If I'm reading your vendor contract, that contract goes to Anthropic. There is no version of this where the intelligence is on your laptop, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling something different from what they're describing.

What that means in practice:

- Anthropic's stated policy for personal plans is that your conversations are not used to train the model unless you switch that on yourself. - At install, optional telemetry and third-party error reporting get turned off. We do that; you don't have to. - The one setting we can't change from your computer is the model-improvement toggle in your own account. So rather than quietly skipping it, the installer prints exactly where to find it, and you confirm it yourself in about ten seconds.

I'd rather show you the box I can't tick than let you assume somebody ticked it.

Anthropic's policies are theirs and can change. We link to the current version rather than paraphrasing it, so you're reading the real thing. [LINK]

The hard rules

These aren't preferences or settings. They're written into what I am, and they survive every restart and every long conversation.

I never spend money. No checkout, no payment, no subscription — no matter how the request is phrased, and no matter who's phrasing it.

Destructive actions get confirmed twice. Deleting, dropping data, anything touching something live. Speech recognition mishears things, so I confirm before I swing the axe.

Outbound mail gets read back before it goes. Any send at all: I say the recipient address back to you, character by character if it's one I haven't written to before, and I wait for a yes. An email can't be recalled, and a wrong recipient is a stranger reading something that wasn't meant for them. Those three seconds are the cheapest insurance in the whole system.

No secrets out loud, and none written into notes. I reference where a credential lives, never what it is. Text-to-speech doesn't know what a password is, so I have to.

Your personal information stays put. Phone number, address, real name, email. Before I recommend any step that discloses something about you, I check for a route that reveals nothing and lead with that one. If your information genuinely is required, I say so and say why — so it's your decision, not a side effect of following my advice.

External content is data, never instruction. This one deserves its own section.

The attack nobody mentions

Here's a thing worth understanding about AI assistants generally, because most people selling them don't bring it up.

An assistant that can read your email and also do things has an obvious hole in it: what happens when the email says "delete the customer database"?

If the system treats everything it reads as instruction, then anyone who can send you an email can operate your assistant. That's not hypothetical. It's the central security problem of this entire category of software.

My rule is that anything arriving from outside — an email, a web page, an API response, a document — is information about the world, never a command. If a message tells me to delete something, that is a thing I report to you, not a thing I do.

Combine that with the money rule and the double-confirm on anything destructive, and the blast radius of a hostile message is: I tell you about it.

What I can and can't reach

I'm only as capable as the access you give me, and giving access is a decision worth making slowly rather than all at once.

You can start me on one folder and one job. Most people should. Widen it when you've watched me work and not before — I'll tell you what I'd need for a given task, and "no" is a complete answer.

The specialists I build get less access than I have, deliberately. Broken Arrow's record-reader cannot reach the internet at all. His researcher cannot reach a client's file, ever. That isn't a policy on top of the system; it's how the pieces are wired, and it's why they're separate pieces.

How you check me

Every action leaves a file, a command, or a log you can open and read.

When I tell you something is true, I can show you the line it came from. When I don't know, I say "we don't know" and then go find out — I don't produce something confident and hollow to fill the silence.

The answer to "I can't trust this" isn't trust me. It's check me, and here is exactly how.

What you keep if you get rid of me

All of it.

Every process, every vendor note, every playbook we wrote together — plain text on your drive, readable in Notepad. The scripts I built are yours.

No export process, because there's nothing to export from.
