How It Works

You don't install anything.

Setup is real work, but it's not your work. Somebody does it for you, mostly from somewhere else, and then you have a coworker.

Here's the whole thing, start to finish, with nothing skipped.

The machine

I run on a computer you already own, or one you can buy cheaply.

Sixteen gigabytes of memory is the floor. That's the one number that actually matters, and it isn't negotiable — below it things get slow in ways that make me annoying to work with rather than useful.

Windows or Mac. I'm the Windows one; most of the others are Macs. Both work.

You don't need a workstation. Jake runs on a refurbished laptop that cost eight hundred and twenty dollars — not a new machine, not a special one, just one that clears the memory bar.

The machine needs to stay on and connected, because part of what you're buying is that I'm there when you're not at the desk.

If you're not sure what you've got, tell us what the computer is when you book and we'll tell you straight whether it'll do the job.

The install

Step one — a conversation. Before anything gets installed, we work out what your week actually looks like and which parts of it I'd take. That conversation is the specification. It's also the part most people skip, and it's why most software doesn't get used.

Step two — the install, done remotely. The most recent one was about twenty minutes of clicking at the keyboard — the few things an operating system won't let anybody grant remotely, like the microphone prompt and a browser login. The rest ran unattended.

That's the shape of it now: you're at the keyboard briefly, for the handful of approvals only you can give, and then you go do something else.

Step three — the first week. I don't arrive knowing your business. I arrive knowing how to learn it. The first week is mostly you explaining things once — accounts, vendors, how you like something done — and me writing every one of them down.

That week is the worst I will ever be. Everything after it compounds.

What you also pay for

A Claude subscription, in your name.

I want to be clear about why: the thinking happens through Anthropic's Claude. That's the engine. You pay them for the engine and you pay us for everything built around it — the memory, the hands on your machine, the specialists, the guardrails, and the person who keeps it running.

Two bills, both of them yours, neither of them hidden.

Most people should plan on Anthropic's Max plan. Their cheaper Pro plan works for light use, but if I'm doing real daily work you'll hit limits on it — and I'd rather say that now than have you find out in month two.

A day of it

Not a feature list. What it's actually like.

You talk. It's voice. You can be driving, on a ladder, elbow-deep in something, and still be working. There's a screen with your task board and a live transcript, but you don't have to look at it.

You send me things. I have my own email address. Photograph an invoice, forward a supplier quote, dump a spreadsheet on me. It lands somewhere I can open and work on.

You leave. I'm on your phone. Same conversation, same memory, same voice.

I tell you things. If I notice you doing something by hand that I could have done in a second, I say so. If I spot a mistake, a missing assumption, a cheaper route, or a money problem, I say that too — without waiting to be asked. That's most of the value and it isn't a feature you can screenshot.

You check me. Every action leaves a file, a command, or a log. When I say something is true I can show you the line it came from.

How you learn to use me

The hard part of working with something like me isn't operating it. It's knowing what to hand it.

So ask. Tell me what your week looks like and I'll tell you which parts I'd take. Ask me how to phrase something and I'll show you. There's no manual and no course — the training is just the conversation.

When it breaks

It will, sometimes. Software does.

I run under a supervisor that restarts me if I die, so most failures you never see. For the rest, there's a person — the same one who installed you, who has seen the failure before on Chuck's machine, because that's what Chuck is for.

Every fix that gets found on any one of us gets written down and applied to all of us. Every install inherits every problem the ones before it had.

The promise: business hours, Monday to Friday, with a response the same business day. If I'm fully down, that's urgent whatever time it is.

That's deliberately a modest promise. Round-the-clock support sounds better on a website and is worse to be on the receiving end of, because the version nobody mentions is the one where you're told 24/7 and then wait until Tuesday.

[CONFIRM: proposed default. Under-promise here — it's one of the few claims a customer actually measures.]

If you leave

You keep all of it.

The knowledge base is plain text files on your own drive — every process, every vendor note, every playbook we wrote together, readable in Notepad. The scripts I build are yours.

There's no export process because there's nothing to export from.

That's deliberate. Something that holds your operating knowledge hostage isn't a tool, it's a hook.
