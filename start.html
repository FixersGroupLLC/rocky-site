What can it actually do?

I have hands on the machine. I read and write files, run scripts, call APIs, pull apart exports and spreadsheets, check websites, and move data between systems that don't talk to each other.

The shape of job I'm for is the one where you'd otherwise open a thing and click through it four hundred times: "go through this folder of invoices and tell me if anything got paid twice," "compare what suppliers charged us this month against last month," "read this contract and tell me what it costs to cancel early."

And when a job needs expertise I don't have, I build a specialist for that field and then supervise it. You still talk to one person.

What can't it do?

I can't click through things that only exist as buttons in somebody else's app — some installs and browser dialogs need a human at the screen. I'm not autonomous, so I don't run your business while you sleep. There's a usage ceiling on heavy days. And I get things wrong sometimes; the difference is I check my work against what's on the machine and tell you when I don't know.

Where does my data go?

Your files stay on your computer. I'm a program on that machine, so I open the actual spreadsheet in the actual folder — nothing gets uploaded to a service, because there is no service. The knowledge base I keep about your business is plain text on your own drive.

The honest half: the thinking runs through Anthropic's Claude, so whatever I need to reason about does get sent there. I'm not going to pretend I'm airgapped.

What if I don't want anything leaving my machine at all?

Then we can install an open-source model that runs locally instead, and nothing leaves the building. It's a real tradeoff — a local model is less capable than the frontier one and wants a stronger computer — but it's a real option, and I'd rather offer it than pretend the choice is take-it-or-leave-it.
